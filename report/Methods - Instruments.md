# Methods - Instruments

## Resistograph

The Resistograph is a micro-drilling device that records drilling resistance as a function of depth, providing a high-resolution proxy for wood density. Because modulus of elasticity (MOE) is strongly correlated with density, resistograph-derived metrics (e.g., mean resistance, peak amplitude, and area under the curve) can be used in calibrated statistical models to estimate MOE. While the method is semi-destructive and requires species-specific calibration, it offers detailed insight into intra-stem variability and is particularly useful for assessing wood quality in standing trees and logs.

## TreeSonic

The TreeSonic is a non-destructive acoustic instrument used to estimate the modulus of elasticity (MOE) of standing trees by measuring the time-of-flight of stress waves along the stem. The device uses two probes inserted into the wood (typically at breast height), where one probe generates a mechanical impact and the other records the arrival time of the wave. Because acoustic velocity in wood is strongly related to stiffness, MOE can be estimated using wood density and the squared wave velocity. While it does not directly measure MOE, it yields reliable estimates when combined with density information and proper calibration. Limitations include sensitivity to probe alignment, moisture content, and the need for species-specific calibration.

## Microsecond

The Microsecond Timer is a non-destructive acoustic device used to estimate the modulus of elasticity (MOE) of wood by measuring the transit time of a stress wave between two sensors. A mechanical impact (typically with a hammer) generates a stress wave that travels through the wood, and the instrument records the travel time with microsecond precision. Similarly to TreeSonic, MOE is estimated as function of squared wave velocity and wood density. It provides reliable MOE estimates when calibrated, but results can be influenced by sensor coupling, grain direction, moisture content, and measurement geometry, requiring consistent protocols and species-specific calibration for accurate predictions. A key difference with TreeSonic is the measurement geometry: Microsecond measures Radial/transverse velocity, while TreeSonic measures longitudinal velocity
